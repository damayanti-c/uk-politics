# Reform councils and spending: data, limits, and findings so far

*How are Reform-run councils spending compared with others, and can we yet tell?*

## a) What data exists to compare Reform councils with others

The comparison is well served at the aggregate level and thin at the granular level.

- **MHCLG Revenue Account (budget) and Revenue Outturn (actuals) returns** are the backbone: standardised, per-authority, per-service revenue spending for every council in England, comparable across authorities and over time. We have built a budget panel (2022/23 to 2026/27) for all Reform-run councils plus matched non-Reform counties as controls.
- **Councils' own budget books, Medium Term Financial Strategies and savings schedules** give line-by-line detail on planned changes. We have coded the full 2026/27 savings schedules of the six Reform-majority counties (375 lines) from their primary budget papers.
- **The £500 transaction data** (every payment over £500) is the only source granular enough to see supplier-level shifts, but it is inconsistent and not yet analysed.

So a like-for-like Reform vs non-Reform comparison is possible now at service-total level, using matched Conservative-held counties as the counterfactual.

## b) What the data can reveal now, and when it can fully answer the question

The binding constraint is timing, not data quality. Councils set budgets in February/March for the April year; Reform won control in May 2025. Therefore:

- **2025/26** was set by the previous administrations before the election. Reform inherited it whole.
- **2026/27** is Reform's first own budget, set February 2026. It is available now, but only as **budgeted intent**, and roughly 95% of it is rolled-forward base (staff, contracts, statutory demand) rather than fresh Reform choices.
- **Actual spending (outturn)** for 2026/27 does not publish until around **September 2027**.

So today we can measure what Reform councils *plan* to spend, not what they *actually* spend. The definitive "did spending change, in level and pattern" question needs outturn: the 2025/26 outturn (a transition year) around September 2026, the first clean Reform year (2026/27) around September 2027, and a second year around September 2028. One complication: local government reorganisation is expected to abolish the treated shire counties around 2027/28, which narrows the clean before-and-after window to essentially one or two years.

## c) Main findings from the data that exists so far

On the available budget and savings data, there is **no reallocation of substance**, and almost none of the current spending is Reform's own choice.

- **Overall level.** Reform councils' total budgets rose about 9-12% into their first budget, in line with prior years and comparable councils. No cash cut.
- **Composition.** The discretionary services a council can actually move (culture, environment, planning, central, highways) are only about 9-10% of service spend, and near-identical in Reform counties (9.4%) and comparable non-Reform counties (9.8%). No visible compositional shift.
- **Savings audit (six counties).** Councils' own stated 2026/27 savings total about £254m, but after stripping out inherited savings, technical and actuarial items, income generation, and demand-avoidance in statutory social care, only about **£30-44m are genuinely new discretionary cuts** across six £1-3bn budgets. There are **zero** EDI or diversity savings lines and only about £0.3m of climate cuts; the real cuts are conventional back-office, property, IT and senior-management efficiencies, plus minor trims to street lighting, gritting and libraries.
- **Compared with non-Reform councils, no different.** Coding five comparable non-Reform counties (Cambridgeshire, Devon, Gloucestershire, Hertfordshire, Oxfordshire) through the identical method gives a like-for-like benchmark. Genuine discretionary cuts are about 0.4% of budget for the Reform councils and 0.3-0.4% for the controls, statistically indistinguishable; the single biggest discretionary cutter of all eleven is a **Liberal Democrat council** (Hertfordshire). The controls make **zero** EDI cuts too.
- **Council tax: Reform cut its rise, but by less than the raw gap suggests.** In 2026/27 the six Reform councils averaged a 3.93% own-precept rise against 4.99% for the five controls. But because a level comparison conflates a Reform effect with pre-existing council character, we ran a difference-in-differences on each council's change from its predecessor's 2025/26 rise. The genuine Reform effect is about **0.7 points**, not the full 1.06-point gap: the controls all held flat at the 4.99% maximum through their own 2025 administration flips, while Reform councils cut their rise by 0.70 points on average. It is concentrated in four councils (Lancashire -1.19, Kent -1.00, Staffordshire -1.00, Nottinghamshire -0.85, all cutting from the cap); Derbyshire and Lincolnshire barely moved (-0.09 each). Lincolnshire's low 2.90% is not a Reform achievement, it was already a 2.99% council under the Conservatives, which is why the raw gap overstates the effect by about a third.

- **Attribution.** The whole of 2025/26 was the predecessors'; 2026/27 is about 95% inherited base, and roughly 16% or more of even the savings layer was explicitly carried over from the previous administration's plans.

**Verdict:** on the intent data available now, the "DOGE" narrative substantially outruns the budgets, consistent with Kent and Staffordshire conceding their efficiency reviews found little, and Reform councils look like comparable non-Reform councils on cuts while taxing residents somewhat less. Whether even these modest changes are delivered is testable only when 2026/27 outturn arrives around September 2027.

*Scope: revenue budgets, not capital; the six majority-run Reform counties and five matched non-Reform controls, not all thirteen Reform-influenced councils; budgeted intent, not actual outturn.*
